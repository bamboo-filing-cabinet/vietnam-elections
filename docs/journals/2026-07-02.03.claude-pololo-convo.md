## Viet: 

what is wikidata?

## claude: 

Wikidata is a free, collaborative knowledge base run by the Wikimedia Foundation (the same organization behind Wikipedia). Where Wikipedia stores human-readable articles, Wikidata stores structured, machine-readable data about the world: people, places, events, concepts, and so on.

The core unit is the *item*, each given a unique identifier like Q42 (Douglas Adams) or Q90 (Paris). Items are described through *statements* that pair a *property* (also identified, e.g. P569 for "date of birth") with a value, and statements can themselves carry qualifiers and source references. So rather than prose, you get assertions like "Q42 — P569 — 11 March 1952" with provenance attached.

A few things that make it notable:

It's fundamentally a graph data model. Items are nodes, properties are typed edges, and values are often other items, so the whole thing forms a large interconnected knowledge graph rather than a set of flat records. You'd recognize the shape immediately given your genealogical-format thinking; it's essentially the same node-and-typed-edge problem you've been chewing on, just at planetary scale and without a domain-specific schema.

It's multilingual by design. Labels and descriptions exist in many languages, but the underlying item identity is language-neutral, which is part of why it serves as a backbone for linking data across Wikipedia editions and other projects.

It's queryable. There's a public SPARQL endpoint (the Wikidata Query Service) that lets you ask graph questions like "all people born in Paris who were physicists," traversing edges across the dataset. It also exports as RDF and slots into the broader linked-open-data / semantic web ecosystem.

For your genealogical project specifically, Wikidata is worth studying both as a model and as a cautionary tale. Its property system for family relationships (P22 father, P25 mother, P26 spouse, P40 child) is deliberately minimal and struggles with exactly the things archival genealogy cares about: uncertainty, conflicting sources, date ranges, and the distinction between a documented claim and an asserted fact. The reification-via-qualifiers approach is how it bolts provenance onto a simple triple model, which is one design path you could borrow or deliberately reject.

I assume P stands for property, what would Q stand for?

## claude: 

Here's the slightly anticlimactic answer: the Q doesn't stand for a word the way P stands for "property." Items are the things Wikidata describes, so the logical letter would have been "I," but that was avoided (it's easily confused with the digit 1 and the lowercase L, and "I"/"item" collides with other conventions). Q was essentially a free, unambiguous letter with no competing meaning, so it was chosen for item identifiers.

The number that follows is just sequential: Q-IDs are assigned consecutively whenever a new item is created, starting with Q1, which was there when Wikidata launched and refers to the universe. So Q42 (Douglas Adams) was roughly the 42nd item created, Q90 (Paris) the 90th, and so on. The combined letter-plus-number string is often called a "QID."

There's also a widely repeated bit of lore that the Q was chosen in honor of Kasia, the wife of Wikidata's founding developer Denny Vrandečić, but I'd treat that as an anecdote rather than the documented official reason. The practical justification was just "an available letter that wouldn't be confused with anything else."

This actually connects to a design decision relevant to your genealogical format: Wikidata deliberately uses *opaque*, meaningless identifiers rather than semantic ones. A person's QID encodes nothing about who they are; all meaning lives in the statements attached to it. That's the opposite of, say, a scheme where you'd build a readable key like `smith-john-1850-london`. The opaque approach trades human readability for stability, since the identifier never has to change when facts, names, or your understanding of the entity change, which matters a lot when you're modeling contested or revised genealogical claims.

Mostly no — and the "standards evolve" point cuts against a new one more than for it, once you look at *how* they evolve.

Start with what a standard actually is, decomposed: a schema, a governance process, and a set of independent adopters. You can author the schema yourself this afternoon. You cannot author the adopters yourself at all. A schema with one implementation isn't a standard — it's your project's data format with ambitious branding. Standards are descriptive before they're prescriptive; they ratify what several parties already do in common. So "do we need a new standard" splits into two very different questions. "Do I need a published schema?" — yes, obviously, it's cheap, do it now. "Should I try to start a new *standard*?" — almost never, and never by declaring one.

For most of your data the XKCD-927 trap is real: solve a fragmentation problem by adding one more competing format. The entity layer — persons, organizations, areas, the membership of winners — already has Popolo, and it fits. Now your actual premise, how standards evolve. Two modes. One is accretion: someone extends a living standard through its governance and the extension becomes canon. Two is displacement: a new standard out-competes an old one, which needs either an institution behind it (W3C, ISO, a government) or organic adoption by many parties. Worth taking seriously — the feature is real — but it has some sharp edges that bear directly on your project. Let me check the current specifics rather than wing it.Your correspondent is right that the mechanism exists, and they've actually put their finger on the one thing that solves a problem I flagged earlier. But it doesn't change the architecture conclusion — it just gives you a better *export target*.

The mechanism is real. Commons has a `Data:` namespace that hosts `.tab` tabular files (CSV-like, stored as JSON with a typed schema), and since 2017 Wikidata has a dedicated `tabular-data` datatype, so you can add a statement on a Wikidata item that links to a dataset on Commons. The Wikimedia community explicitly frames this as the sanctioned escape hatch: Commons tabular data is positioned as an alternative to Wikidata for time series, numerical data, or data only available under a CC-BY or CC-BY-SA licence, with the intent that you then link and standardize it using Wikidata identifiers.

That licensing clause is the genuinely useful part for you. Wikidata's main namespace is CC0-only — no license other than CC0 is compatible with its copyright requirements, which is why pouring your data straight into Wikidata was always going to clash with your CC-BY-4.0 dataset. But the Commons `Data:` namespace allows a broader set: CC0, the various CC-BY and CC-BY-SA versions, ODbL, and a couple of others. So Commons tabular data can legally host your returns *with attribution intact*, which Wikidata proper cannot. Your correspondent threaded the needle there, probably without knowing it.

Now the sharp edges, and they're the same three themes from this whole conversation:

It's still a mutable wiki page, not an archive. A `.tab` page has edit history but anyone can change it, and it isn't dated, tamper-evident, or source-faithful in the way an archive must be. So this is a *distribution and linkage* layer, not a system of record. Your git repo stays the filing cabinet.

The format is flat, so your provenance doesn't survive. The `.tab` schema is typed columns and rows with a single free-text `sources` field for the whole table — there's no per-cell `fetched_date`, no per-document `terms_status`, no nested `sources[]`/`annotations[]`. Your "won" derivations and per-field citations — the entire differentiator of your project — would be flattened away. You'd be publishing a thin, citable table, not your archival-grade data.

And the practical surface is finicky and a bit unstable: pages are capped (2 MB, basic types only) and edited as raw JSON or via a CSV import gadget, and the rendering tooling around it (the old Graph extension, now the 2025 Charts extension) has churned over the years.

So the move that actually makes sense: keep your repo as the canonical, provenance-rich, immutable-per-snapshot source of truth — the thing the schema now guards — and treat a Commons `.tab` file as a *flattened derivative export*, CC-BY-4.0, whose `sources`/`description` fields point back at your repo and JSON Schema for the full provenance. Then link that dataset from the relevant Wikidata items (province, election) via the tabular-data property. That gets you discoverability and Wikidata interoperability without pretending Commons is your archive, and it degrades gracefully: people who want the citable numbers get them on Commons, people who want the sourced claims follow the link home. It's the same pattern as before — graph for entities, link out for returns — with Commons slotted in as one more downstream mirror, exactly where AP's feed or your own `public/data` exports already sit.
